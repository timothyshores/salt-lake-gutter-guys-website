# Ethics, working agreement, and what we do if a client stiffs us

Written 2026-08-01. Tim and Brandon. Update as the arrangement evolves.

## The working agreement (informal, and that's fine for now)

**How it started:** Brandon does part-time in-person lead gen for a roofing
company and had been watching agency-building content online (mostly
GoHighLevel-flavored). Tim mentioned he was building a web app with Claude Code
while between jobs. Brandon asked if he could build websites, Tim said yes, and
Brandon proposed: **if he finds clients who pay, Tim does the work, and they
figure out the split later.**

**Division of labor, as actually practiced:**

| Brandon | Tim |
|---|---|
| Cold calling and dialing | Everything technical |
| Sales, pitching, objection handling | Build, hosting, deployment |
| Closing | Lead capture, automation |
| Client relationship and account management | Revisions and delivery |
| Collecting revision requests | Research and pricing analysis |

Brandon is the only client-facing role. Tim does not talk to clients unless
Brandon wants him on a call.

**Both are doing this in their spare time.** It is a side hustle for both, and
that is the correct speed - nobody quits anything until it produces real revenue.

## The unresolved item: the revenue split

**Still undefined.** It was explicitly deferred at the start ("we'll figure out
splits later") and has not been revisited.

That was reasonable when the answer was $0. **It stops being reasonable the
moment client #1 pays.** Splitting hypothetical money is easy; splitting real
money that has already landed in someone's account is where partnerships break.

**Action: settle the split before the first client's money changes hands.** Not
earlier - there is nothing to divide yet. Not later - the first payment is the
forcing function.

Things worth deciding at that moment, none of which need a lawyer:
- Percentage split on the $500 site, and on the monthly retainer (they may differ
  - the site is mostly Tim's labor, the monthly is mostly Brandon's retention work)
- Who fronts costs (domains, Formspree Professional, Twilio)
- What happens if one person stops participating
- Whether either can take a client solo

Write it in a shared doc. An email both parties agree to beats a handshake.

## Lead ownership - the rule

**Leads generated by a client's website belong to that client. Full stop.**

Selling Jason's leads to another gutter company would be selling him out to his
own competitor, using a site he paid for. That is the thing that ends the
business.

### The subtler trap: we keep copies

Every lead currently lands in Tim's inbox, and will keep landing there after
clients are added as recipients (deliberately - it is how we prove delivery when
a client says "I never got that").

**With eight gutter clients, that inbox is a database of homeowners who want
gutter work.** Monetizing it in any direction - selling it, remarketing to it,
routing overflow to another client - is the same betrayal in nicer clothes.

**The rule: our copies exist for delivery verification and nothing else.**
Written down now, while it costs nothing to agree to.

Tim should route these to a Gmail label/filter so they do not clutter his inbox.
Filtering is fine. Deleting them is not - they are the delivery receipt.

## If a client does not pay

The concern is legitimate: a client stiffs us, the card declines, they stop
answering. What is our recourse?

### What we do NOT do: resell their leads

Considered and rejected. The reason is not squeamishness, it is that **the leads
are not the client's property to seize - they are the homeowner's personal
information.**

A homeowner typed their name and phone into what they believed was Salt Lake
Gutter Guys' contact form. They consented to *that company* calling them.
Routing them to three competitors is a use they never agreed to, and it happens
regardless of whether the contractor paid us. **The client wronged us. The
homeowner did not.**

Practical exposure on top of the ethics:
- Contractors in one metro talk. "They sold my customer list over a billing
  dispute" is the story that ends a local referral business.
- It likely creates TCPA exposure for whoever we sold to, since they are calling
  people who never consented to them.

### What we DO instead - faster, cleaner, no lawyer needed

1. **Turn the site off.** We own the repo and the hosting. Non-payment means the
   site goes dark. Immediate, total, completely defensible - we stopped providing
   a service they stopped paying for.
2. **Stop lead delivery.** Remove their email from the Formspree form. Leads stop
   arriving. Takes 30 seconds.
3. **Keep the domain in the client's name** (already our standing recommendation).
   We never hold their property hostage - that is what creates legal risk for
   *us*.
4. **Take money up front.** $500 before launch. Monthly auto-billed on a card.
   The best collections strategy is not extending credit to a stranger.

Turning off the site is stronger leverage than reselling leads, because it is
instant and needs no justification.

## The directory idea - a real business, but separate

Tim raised building our own "Salt Lake City Gutters" style directory site -
essentially Angi's model, our own site collecting our own leads and routing them
to contractors.

**That is a legitimate business.** Homeowners contacting a directory know they
are contacting a directory; a directory routing to multiple contractors is the
product working as advertised. Angi does exactly this.

**It just cannot be built from client-captured leads.** Our own site, our own
traffic, our own consent language - clean. Repurposing leads captured under a
client's brand - not clean, regardless of whether the client paid.

Worth revisiting as a product later. Not a fallback threat, and not a plan for
this year.

## Reporting to clients - do this, it is the retention tool

**Send every paying client a monthly lead report.** Not because they asked, but
because it is the single cheapest thing that keeps them paying.

Recall from `MARKET-PRICING.md`: 43% of small-business marketing churn happens in
the first 90 days, and **price is only the 6th most-cited reason clients leave**,
behind weak strategic guidance (68%) and poor communication (57%). Clients do not
churn because it costs too much. They churn because nothing seems to be happening.

A monthly report is the artifact that proves something is happening.

**What to send, minimum:**
- Leads received this month
- What each one asked for (the form already captures service type and urgency)
- Month-over-month comparison once there is history

**What makes it powerful once we have a few months of data:** we can quantify
our own value. "Your site generated 11 leads last month. At your typical job
value, that's roughly $X in pipeline, for $150." That sentence is worth more
than any feature.

**And it compounds into sales.** Real numbers from real clients become the pitch
for the next client, in gutters or any other trade: "our contractor sites average
N leads a month." That is a materially stronger cold-call opening than "we build
websites."

**Build it when there is a paying client to send it to.** Formspree's dashboard
has the submission count today; a manual count and a short email beats no report,
and beats building a reporting system nobody is paying for yet.
